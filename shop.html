Shop: from PDF catalogues to a store merchants run themselves

I was asked to build a shop. What merchants actually needed was a way to stop taking every order by hand, without the cost and setup of Shopify or Wix.

RoleProduct Management Intern
TimelineJan – Aug 2026
My partResearch, PRD, scope, flows, testing, pilot onboarding
TeamFounders, Designer, Engineering, Sales & Ops
In 30 seconds
  • Problem: small sellers ran every order by hand across PDF catalogues, chats and UPI screenshots.
  • Research: 44 merchant interviews. The barrier to going online was setup effort, cost and payments, not desire.
  • Decision: V1 scoped to one complete order, with UPI-ID payments instead of a gateway, and growth tools moved to V2.
  • Result: live with 10 pilot merchants. I have no verified usage numbers, so I don't show any.

01Context

SocialAddress is an AI-powered link-in-bio and WhatsApp business platform. Small businesses and creators use it to run their links, bookings, posts and customer conversations from one mobile page.

Many of them already sent customers to that page from their Instagram bio. The founder set the next step: let them sell there too. My job was to figure out what to build and how, then take it to merchants.

02Where merchants started

The pilot merchants were existing SocialAddress clients. Most had no website. A typical order looked like this:

PDF catalogueShared on chat
Product codeCustomer picks one
Back-and-forthPrice, size, delivery
UPI + screenshotMerchant checks by hand
DeliveryArranged separately

They already had customers. What slowed them down was everything after someone said "I want to buy this."

03What 44 merchant interviews showed

I interviewed 44 merchants: 39 product sellers and 5 sellers of workshops and digital offerings. I didn't ask what features they wanted. I asked about their workflow, starting with how they sold their last product.

Observed

They want a store, but setup stops them

Several had started a Shopify site and given up, or handed it to a developer, because of themes, variants, product uploads and payment setup.

Interpretation

The barrier is effort, not desire

So the design goal became a store a merchant could set up in about five minutes, without a developer. This was a goal, not a measured time.

Counter-signal

Shop isn't for everyone. 12 of 39 sellers described businesses a fixed store doesn't suit: made-to-order products, prices that change, seasonal stock, wholesale buyers, or fear of designs being copied. Shop fits sellers with standard, ready-stock products best. Custom and wholesale sellers need a quote or catalogue flow instead.

04The idea merchants said no to

To keep things simple, my first idea was fixed, ready-made templates where merchants only add products. When I showed it to merchants, they said it would be easy to start but wouldn't help them. They wanted the store to look like their brand, because customers trust something that looks like a real website more than buying through DMs.

That changed how I saw Shop. It had to make selling easier, and make the merchant look trustworthy. So V1 became: pick a template, then add your own logo, colours and fonts.

05V1: one real order, start to finish

Every V1 feature had to answer one question: can a merchant complete one real order without leaving the platform?

Branded storeTemplate, logo, colours
ProductsPhysical and digital, with inventory
OrderDelivery-zone check
PaymentMerchant's UPI ID
DeliveryOwn zones or Shiprocket
UpdatesAuto messages, invoice, reviews

Trade-off: UPI instead of a payment gateway

Merchants already took payment by UPI, and some named payment setup as one of the "technical" parts of a website. Requiring a Razorpay account would add a step they don't take today. So V1 asked only for a UPI ID. I turned the payment gateway's documentation into requirements with engineering.

What it gained

No gateway setup, so merchants could start selling straight away.

What it cost

Payments went to SocialAddress and were paid out to the merchant within 24 hours. Merchants waited for their money, and payouts were manual work for the team. That's fine for a pilot, but it would need to change at scale.

Merchants weren't asked about gateways directly. This decision is my interpretation of how they described taking payment.

What waited for V2

Coupons, targeted discounts, abandoned-cart reminders and campaigns. A few merchants did ask for these, but they help merchants grow sales. They don't matter until a merchant has made a first sale.

07The pilot and what changed

Shop went live with 10 pilot merchants. Feedback was generally positive, and the main theme was that they could set up and run the store themselves. This was a qualitative pilot. My internship ended before longer-term results were tracked, so I don't show usage, order or revenue numbers.

Three friction points came up: building the store still felt like effort, adding products took time, and writing product descriptions was hard.

In testing

AI-written product descriptions

We connected the SAM AI agent to product listing. A merchant describes a product in plain words, and SAM drafts the title and description. I tested it by acting as a merchant, found where the output went wrong, and shared fixes with engineering. Some pilot merchants were using it before I left.

Done

Making behaviour visible

Alongside the pilot, I defined analytics requirements and set up event tracking across Shop. The team could then see what merchants did, not only what they said on calls.

08What I learned

  • The request isn't always the problem. I was asked for a shop. What merchants needed was to stop running every order by hand.
  • Show something before building it. My simplest idea, fixed templates, was wrong. I only found out because I showed it to merchants first.
  • Define success before the pilot. Next time I'd set metrics upfront (store published, time to first product, first order) and time the five-minute goal with real merchants. Then the pilot would produce evidence, not just feedback.
